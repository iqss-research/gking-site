{{ .Title }}

Gary King and Katalina Toth

Professional researchers are public figures and essentially all have websites, usually beginning in graduate school. If you are either starting out your academic career and want a website like Katalina's (a post-bac at Harvard's IQSS), or need a more extensive one like Gary's (as a post-post-post-…bac. faculty member and director of IQSS), then follow directions below to build your first version in 15 minutes — from scratch or starting with your existing page. Really, it's easy; further customization is simple; hosting is free; the site is lightning fast; and, although it uses all the best modern tools, you don't need to know any of them. Sites built here are also designed to be AI-ready: search engines and AI assistants (ChatGPT, Claude, and the like) can read, index, and correctly cite you and your work. We built this for our own webpages; suggestions welcome.

Get your site up and running

This section is all you need. Just follow the steps — it takes about 15 minutes. Click any step to expand it.

★ Before you begin — what you'll need ▾
  • An AI agent. Any good AI agent will work — you need an agent, not a browser chat window like ChatGPT. Not sure which to pick?
    • Comfortable with the command line or programming? Use Cursor (what we use), Claude Code, or OpenAI's Codex.
    • Prefer no code or terminal? Try Claude Cowork — Anthropic's desktop app that works right on your computer.
    All of these require a paid plan: with Cursor, create a free account and pick a plan that includes Claude (Pro or above); Claude Code, Claude Cowork, and Codex also require paid plans.
  • A GitHub account — sign up free at github.com if you don't have one. GitHub is a free service that stores and publishes your website. It uses version control, meaning every change is saved as a checkpoint you can revisit or undo — so nothing is ever lost, and several people can safely work on the same site without overwriting each other.
  • A few files (optional) — your CV PDF, a headshot, and any paper PDFs you'd like hosted.
1 Get your information form ▾

Download the form that fits where you are in your career, then fill in your details:

{{ range $prompts }} {{ upper (strings.TrimPrefix "." (path.Ext .file)) }} {{ .name }}{{ .meta }} ↓ {{ end }}

The forms are plain-text .txt files. You can open and edit them in any text editor — TextEdit, Notepad, Word, or anything else — and just save when you're done. No special software needed.

The form asks for information about you. You have two options:

  • Building from scratch: fill in your information, publications, software, etc.
  • Migrating an existing site: skip the long fields — just paste the URL of your current website into the form's website field, and the assistant will pull the content from it.
2 Create a GitHub repository ▾

Go to github.com, click the + icon in the top right, and select New repository. Name it something like yourname-site or yourusername.github.io. Set it to Public. Don't add any files — leave it empty.

3 Open your AI assistant and give it your form ▾

Open whichever assistant you chose and start a new conversation:

  • Cursor: open it and press Cmd+I (Mac) or Ctrl+I (Windows).
  • Claude Code or Codex: open it in a new, empty folder on your computer.

Then attach your filled-out form (or paste its contents), along with any PDFs/photos, and say:

"Create a new academic website for [Your Name] using the attached information form, into the GitHub repository at https://github.com/YOURUSERNAME/REPONAME. Build it and show me a preview before anything goes public."

The assistant will build your site — this may take a few minutes for large sites.

4 Preview and approve ▾

The assistant gives you a private preview link like localhost:1313. Open it to browse your site.

Good to know This preview runs locally — only on your own computer. No one else can see it and nothing is public yet. Your site doesn't go live on the internet until you publish it in the next step.

If your site has a People page, the assistant builds it automatically from your co-authors and guesses a link for each one by searching the web — so a few may point to the wrong person or page. Skim those links while previewing and ask the assistant to fix any that are off.

This tool uses AI to generate a website tailored to you — different from any other site created with the same tool. If you don't like some of the AI's design choices, no matter how small or large, you can fix them by merely explaining to your agent what you'd prefer.

To keep every site different, your site's look is drawn at random and then refined, using a method from GaryKing.org/quest. Your assistant shows you three previews of your home page, each from a different draw, and builds the one you pick — or ask for a mix, or a fresh set of three.

Tell the assistant what to fix — layout, missing content, ordering, anything — and iterate until you're happy. You can talk to it like a person designing your site.

5 Publish it ▾

When you're happy, just say:

"Looks good. Commit and push to main."

The assistant publishes to GitHub, which builds and hosts the site automatically. Your site goes live within a few minutes. Want a nicer web address like yourname.harvard.edu? See "Change your web address" below.

Keep handy for later

Optional — handy once your site is live; you don't need any of this to get started. One thing worth doing: share your name and email so we can reach out about improvements and warn you about any changes that could affect your live site (like hosting limits or dependency updates) — and, if you'd like, list your site in our directory to help it get discovered.

@ Change your web address (use a custom URL) ▾

Your free site lives at an address like yourusername.github.io. You can point a nicer, custom address at it — two common options:

  • Use your university's address. Many universities will give you something like yourname.harvard.edu. Ask your department or IT group to set it up — they'll add a DNS record that points to your site.
  • Buy your own address. Purchase a domain like yourname.com from a registrar such as Namecheap, Cloudflare, or Squarespace (usually about $10–15 a year).

Either way, once you have the address, just tell your assistant:

"Set up a custom domain for my site pointing to [your address, e.g. yourname.harvard.edu], and tell me exactly which DNS records to add."

It will configure the site and hand you the exact records to paste into your university's form or your registrar's dashboard.

+ After it's live: common update prompts ▾

Every update works the same way: pick one of the prompts below or write one of your own, then always finish with the publish step once everything looks good. Each prompt shows you a preview first.

1 Choose an update:

  • Add a journal article
    "Add a new journal article to my site: Title: [title] · Authors: [names] · Venue: [e.g., American Political Science Review, 111, 3, Pp. 484–501] · Year: [year] · Abstract: [paste] · DOI or URL: [link] · Topic: [research area]. The article PDF is attached. Show me a preview first."
  • Add a custom short URL
    "Add a new short URL redirect: Short path: /myproject (becomes yoursite.com/myproject) · Destination: [full URL or site path]. Show me a preview first."
  • Update a person's profile (group sites)
    "Update [Name]'s profile: change their title to [new title] and replace their photo with the attached image. Show me a preview first."
  • Add a research area
    "Add a new research area called '[Name]' with this description: [1–2 sentences]. Assign these existing publications to it: [list titles]. Show me a preview first."
  • Add a presentation or talk
    "Add a new talk: Title: [title] · Event: [venue] · Date: [date] · Slides PDF attached. Show me a preview first."
  • Add Google Analytics

    Google Analytics is a free tool that shows how many people visit your site and which pages they view. When you set it up at analytics.google.com, it gives you a measurement ID — a short code like G-XXXXXXXXXX that links your site to your account. Paste that ID in below.

    "Add Google Analytics to my site. My measurement ID is [G-XXXXXXXXXX]. Show me a preview first."
  • Remove the footer credit
    "Remove the footer credit line."
  • Give your site a different look

    Every site built with mysite gets its own look, drawn at random and then refined (the method is from GaryKing.org/quest). If you'd like another draw:

    "Run the design quest at https://gking.harvard.edu/mysite/files/QUEST_DESIGN.md and show me three previews of a new look. Then build the one I pick."

2 Publish — for whichever option you chose above, once the preview looks right:

"Commit, pull, and push."

This saves your change, pulls in any edits other people have made to main first, and then publishes — so no one's work gets overwritten.

! If something goes wrong ▾
  • Site isn't updating after publishing: go to your repository on GitHub, click the Actions tab. If there's a red X, copy the error and tell your assistant: "The build failed. Here's the error: [paste]."
  • You don't like a recent change: tell your assistant "Revert the last change and republish." Everything is versioned, so nothing is lost forever.
  • Your site is over 1 GB and won't publish: GitHub Pages — the free host these instructions use — caps a published site at about 1 GB.

    You are very unlikely to ever hit this. A normal academic site — text, a headshot, and even a few hundred PDFs — is only tens of megabytes. You'd need on the order of a thousand large PDFs, or hundreds of high-resolution images, to get close to 1 GB. If you do see a build error about the size limit, you can move to Cloudflare Pages — another free host, with far more room — without changing how you work day‑to‑day:

    1. Make a free Cloudflare account at dash.cloudflare.com/sign-up.
    2. Create a Pages project: in the dashboard, go to Workers & Pages → Create → Pages and choose Upload assets (direct upload). Give it a name and finish the short setup — you'll get a free address like yourname.pages.dev.
    3. Copy two keys from Cloudflare — your Account ID and an API token (with the "Cloudflare Pages → Edit" permission) — and add them to your GitHub repository under Settings → Secrets and variables → Actions, named CLOUDFLARE_ACCOUNT_ID and CLOUDFLARE_API_TOKEN.
    4. Let your assistant finish the switch.
      "Update my deploy workflow to also publish the built site to Cloudflare Pages (project name [your project name]) using the CLOUDFLARE_API_TOKEN and CLOUDFLARE_ACCOUNT_ID secrets. Keep GitHub Pages publishing too for now. Show me a preview first."
      It handles all the technical configuration.
    5. Point your web address at Cloudflare. If you use a custom domain (like yourname.university.edu), your university's IT/DNS team makes a one-line change to send it to the new Cloudflare address. If you use the free username.github.io address, you can simply start sharing the new .pages.dev address instead.

    Nothing about editing changes: you still edit and publish exactly as before. Cloudflare just serves the finished site, with far more room to grow.
